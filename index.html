The Story of Mūsā AS and al-Khiḍr

The Story of Mūsā AS and al-Khiḍr: The Limits of Intellect Before Divine Revelation One of the clearest demonstrations that human reason cannot independently and infallibly determine the complete moral and legal status of every particular act is found in the Qur’ānic account of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr. Abū Manṣūr al-Māturīdī himself establishes an important epistemological principle when discussing the limitations of human intellects: “The fundamental principle is that intellects are finite creations which fall short of comprehending the totality of things, and understandings are deficient in attaining the ultimate reality of matters.… Whoever seeks to comprehend them, or to attain the wisdom of Lordship without an indication from Him, wrongs the intellect and burdens it with that which he knows it is incapable of.” — al-Māturīdī, Taʾwīlāt Ahl al-Sunna, vol. 4, p. 457. This principle becomes particularly evident in the story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr. When al-Khiḍr killed the boy, Mūsā immediately objected: “Have you killed an innocent soul without [its having killed] another soul? You have certainly done a terrible thing.” Qur’ān 18:74 From everything that was outwardly apparent to Mūsā, the act was impermissible. The boy appeared to be an innocent person, no offence warranting his execution had been publicly established, and Mūsā had not been given any revealed command permitting him to be killed. Ibn Baṭṭāl makes the epistemological significance of this episode particularly explicit. He writes that Mūsā’s objection was correct according to what was outwardly apparent, and that Mūsā was not blameworthy for making the objection. Yet when al-Khiḍr subsequently disclosed the concealed reality behind his action, the matter appeared differently: “Mūsā’s denial of al-Khiḍr for making a hole in the ship and killing the boy was correct according to what was outwardly apparent, and Mūsā was not blameworthy for that. Then, when al-Khiḍr explained its reason and meaning, the correctness that had appeared to Mūsā in his objection became error, and the error that had appeared to Mūsā in al-Khiḍr’s action became correctness.” — Ibn Baṭṭāl, Sharḥ Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 1:200. The Qur’ān then reveals the information that had been hidden from Mūsā: “As for the boy, his parents were believers, and We feared that he would overwhelm them with transgression and disbelief. So We desired that their Lord should replace him with one better than him in purity and nearer in affection. And I did not do it on my own authority.” Qur’ān 18:80–82 The final statement is especially significant: “And I did not do it on my own authority.” Al-Khiḍr therefore did not simply follow a personal moral intuition or exercise an independent judgment contrary to revelation. His action rested upon a divine command and knowledge of circumstances concealed from Mūsā. This gives us an important distinction. Mūsā judged according to the outwardly available evidence. Given what he knew, his objection was entirely understandable and, as Ibn Baṭṭāl explicitly states, correct according to the apparent circumstances. But Mūsā did not possess all of the relevant information concerning the boy, his future effect upon his believing parents, and the divine wisdom connected with his death. Once the concealed information and divine authorization were disclosed, the apparent assessment could no longer be treated as the complete ruling concerning the matter. Thus, the episode demonstrates that there is a difference between: what appears to the intellect from the information available to it; and the complete reality of a matter as known and commanded by Allah. This is precisely where the limitation of finite human intellect becomes relevant. The intellect may recognize general principles, reason from available evidence, and judge according to what is apparent. But it cannot claim exhaustive knowledge of every hidden fact, every future consequence, every particular circumstance, and every aspect of divine wisdom. If those hidden realities can affect the ruling of a particular matter, then unaided intellect cannot make itself the final and infallible judge over revelation. Ibn Baṭṭāl therefore draws a broader theological principle from the story: Whatever Allah has made binding upon His servants through His religion and Law must be authoritative over the intellects; the intellects are not themselves to be made the authority over it. He then warns against making the finite intellect the ultimate judge over matters whose realities it cannot encompass. Created intellects have an end, whereas Allah’s knowledge has no end: “They encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills.” Qur’ān 2:255 The argument is therefore not that reason is useless, irrational, or incapable of recognizing anything good or evil. Rather, the argument is that reason is not omniscient. Its judgments concerning particular acts depend upon the information available to it. Revelation, however, may disclose a fact, command, exception, or divine wisdom that the intellect could not have reached independently. The story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr provides an unusually powerful illustration of this principle. Mūsā’s initial judgment was based upon the outward reality available to him; al-Khiḍr possessed additional divinely disclosed knowledge. The difference was therefore not that Mūsā suddenly became irrational and al-Khiḍr suddenly became rational. Rather, the epistemic circumstances were different. This is also consistent with al-Māturīdī’s own admission that finite intellects cannot comprehend the totality of things or attain the ultimate realities and divine wisdoms independently of divine indication. If the intellect itself is acknowledged to be finite, then it cannot legitimately transform its own limited assessment into an infallible standard by which every divine command must be judged. Accordingly, when someone argues: “Allah could not command this particular act because my intellect determines that it is intrinsically immoral,” the question that must first be asked is: How do you know that your intellect possesses all the facts and circumstances relevant to the divine ruling? The story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr demonstrates precisely why such certainty cannot simply be assumed. Mūsā saw an apparently innocent person being killed and objected according to the outward evidence. Yet a concealed divine command and concealed knowledge altered the complete understanding of the event. Therefore, the proper conclusion is not that revelation contradicts sound reason. Rather, it is that finite reason cannot claim exhaustive knowledge of every particular act or every divine wisdom, and consequently cannot establish itself as an infallible judge over revelation. The intellect has a place: it understands, reasons, recognizes evidence, and receives revelation. But when revelation establishes something whose complete wisdom lies beyond its apprehension, the proper response is not to reject the revelation because the intellect has failed to comprehend its entirety. The proper response is to recognize the limitation of the intellect itself. This is the fundamental lesson Ibn Baṭṭāl extracts from the story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr what appears irrational or impermissible to a finite intellect on the basis of incomplete information cannot thereby be declared impossible for Allah to command, particularly when revelation supplies the information and authority that the intellect itself lacked.

<p>&nbsp;1. The Core Distinction: Creator vs. Acquirer</p><p>The first step is to establish that every action has two "facets." When Allah brings that action into existence following that intent, that is khalq.</p><p><br /></p><p>The Ruling: Praise, blame, reward, and punishment are attached to the Acquisition, not the creation. Allah creates the "movement," but the human provides the "intent" (e.g., the hand's movement is created by God, but the choice to use it for charity or theft belongs to the human).</p><p><br /></p><p>2. The "Partial Will": Where Freedom Lives</p><p>A common question is: "If God created my will, how am I free?" The answer lies in the Partial Will (al-irāda al-juz’iyya) or Sincere Resolve (‘azm muṣammam),</p><p><br /></p><p>We distinguish between "Inclination" (which can be created and influenced) and "Intention" (the actual decision).</p><p><br /></p><p>This "Partial Will" is considered a Relational State (amr i’tibārī) or a State (ḥāl). It is neither a physically "existent thing" nor "nothing"; it is a point of entry (madkhal) that does not fall under the category of "creation" (khalq) in the material sense.</p><p><br /></p><p>Because this resolve is not a "thing" created by God, it is the one area where the human is truly independent and held responsible. This exists before the action and is the basis for being held responsible (taklīf).</p><p><br /></p><p>Actual Capacity: This is the specific power to execute a specific act. This is created by Allah simultaneously with the action (ma’a al-fi‘l).</p><p><br /></p><p>Allah creates the power to do good if the slave intends good, and the power to do evil if the slave intends evil.&nbsp;</p><p>Thus, the human "wastes" the potential for good by choosing the resolve for evil.</p><p><br /></p><p>4. Knowledge is not Compulsion</p><p>The final hurdle is usually the argument: "If God knew eternally I would do this, I must do it."</p><p>In our theology, Knowledge is a Follower (tābi‘) of the thing known. God knows what will happen as it will happen,</p><p><br /></p><p>He knows that you will perform a certain act by your own choice. If He knew you were forced, and you weren't, His knowledge would be "ignorance," which is impossible.</p><p>Knowledge is a "disclosure" (inkishāf), not a "necessitating cause" (‘illa mūjiba). Knowing a car will crash because its brakes are cut doesn't mean your knowledge caused the crash.</p><p><br /></p><p>Allah has given us the capacity to do all He has made a duty. <p>&nbsp;<span style="font-size: medium;">Biography of Sultan Al Hind:</span></p><p><br /></p><p>Hazrat Khwaja Muinuddin Hasan Chishti is known throughout the country as Khwaja Gharib Nawaz or the friend of the poor.</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">Date and Place of Birth:</span></p><p>Khwaja Saheb was born in the town of Sanjar in Sistan on the 18th April 1142 A.D.</p><p>Other sources indicate he was born at Chisht in Sistan, which was also known as Sajistan, located in East Persia.</p><p>The exact date of birth is a subject of discussion among historians, but the majority of reliable sources support a date between 535 A.H. and 537 A.H.</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">Parents and Ancestry:</span></p><p><br /></p><p>His father was Syed Ghiyasuddin Hasan, and his mother was Syeda Bibi Ummul-wara, also known as Bibi Mah-e-Noor.</p><p><br /></p><p>His parents were directly descended from the Holy Prophet of Islam.</p><p>His paternal lineage traces from Hazrat Imam Husain, the younger son of Hazrat Ali Karam-Allah Wajahu.</p><p>His maternal genealogy traces from Hazrat Imam Hasan, the elder son of Maula Ali (KAW).</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">Miracle reported took place during his boyhood:</span></p><p>Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti asked a shepherd boy whose cow was still immature (not yet of milking age) for some milk.</p><p>The boy responded that all the young cows were only of tree age and were too young to yield milk.</p><p>Khwaja Saheb smiled and said, "I would like to drink the milk of that cow," and proceeded to milk it himself.</p><p>He filled several receptacles with milk, and about 40 people drank to their full satisfaction.</p><p><br /></p><p>According to historians, Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti, even during his childhood, gave an early promise of his rare piety and sacrifice for others. One instance recorded describes him as "the infant saint of the future"</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">He received his initial early education and training from his father.</span></p><p><br /></p><p>He received instruction to ensure he could, at the very least, recite the Holy Quran and learned Quranic lessons at home by the age of seven. As he grew to manhood, he made contact with various leading scholars of the Middle East and developed a profound mark in scholarship.</p><p>His extensive intellectual pursuits included studying the oriental sciences and literature. In Bukhara, he attended many institutions and thoroughly studied subjects like Theology, Philosophy, and Grammar. One of his prominent teachers was Maulana Husamuddin Bokhari, from whom Khwaja Muinuddin received the two highest academic diplomas, known as 'Jalok' and 'Dastar-e-Fazilat' (robes of learning).</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">Personal Tragedy and Renunciation.</span></p><p><br /></p><p>At the age of 15 (551 A.H.), Khwaja Muinuddin experienced a pivotal tragedy when he saw his father die, followed shortly by the loss of his mother. He was left alone in a world marked by "hate, murder, and greed".</p><p>Despite this sorrow, he inherited enough material resources to sustain himself, including a windmill (or mill) and an orchard.</p><p>Overwhelmed by the "ghastly massacre in Khorasan" and the general state of the world, his interest in worldly life and pleasures began diminishing. He felt intense worry and a deep longing to be away from the chaotic world.</p><p>In response to this inner turmoil, he decided to divest himself of all his rich traditions and material possessions, immediately changing into a dervish’s coarse clothing (rags) after receiving a profound divine vision.</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">The Spiritual Catalyst (Encounter with Sheikh Ibrahim Qandozi).</span></p><p><br /></p><p>The spiritual transformation that led to his full renunciation occurred around the year 551 A.H.</p><p>One day, while watering the plants in his orchard, he met a divine person constantly engrossed in communion with God, the dervish Sheikh Ibrahim Qandozi (or Qanduzi).</p><p><br /></p><p>Khwaja Muinuddin welcomed the dervish and seated him under a shady tree, presenting him with a bunch of fresh grapes.</p><p>Deeply impressed by Khwaja Muinuddin’s purity, hospitality, and divine love, the dervish chewed a piece of oil-cake (khak-e-khurul) and put it into the young man's mouth.</p><p>Immediately, Khwaja Muinuddin experienced an extraordinary spiritual manifestation. He was instantly lifted from his worldly imagination and found himself in a "divine concentration".</p><p><br /></p><p>Following this experience, he completely renounced the world. He gave away the whole of his orchard and windmill along with all his worldly belongings, distributing every pie of the proceeds among the poor and the needy, and set out on his journey to find a spiritual master.</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">Becoming a Disciple of Khwaja Usman Harooni.</span></p><p>After renouncing the world and embarking on his spiritual quest, Khwaja Muinuddin's travels took him toward the town of Harwan or Haroon.</p><p>It was there that he met Hazrat Khwaja Usman Harooni, who became his Pir-o-Murshid (spiritual preceptor/master).</p><p>Khwaja Muinuddin remained in his master’s constant company and underwent a rigorous period of training and spiritual development. This involved 20 years of attendance on and travel with his spiritual master. During these long journeys, Khwaja Muinuddin personally carried his master’s bedding, drinking water, and food.</p><p><br /></p><p>The twenty years Hazrat Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti spent traveling and serving under his spiritual guide, Hazrat Khwaja Usman Harooni (his Pir-o-Murshid), constituted the most intense period of his spiritual training and apprenticeship.</p><p><br /></p><p>During these extensive travels, Khwaja Muinuddin undertook humble and continuous service (Khidma) to his master, acting as a personal attendant and student.</p><p>The Path to Divinity: His ultimate goal during this training was to gain perfection in the observation of 'Shari'at' (Islamic law) through proper conduct ('Tariqat'), which leads ultimately to 'Haqiqat' (Divinity or Reality).</p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">The Miracle of Crossing the Tigris.</span></p><p>One notable event occurred during these travels, demonstrating the spiritual power developed through this discipleship:</p><p>When traveling with Khwaja Usman Harooni, they came upon the overflowing Tigris River. Khwaja Muinuddin was worried about the crossing.</p><p>Khwaja Usman asked him to recite 'Surah Ikhlas' one thousand times and close his eyes.</p><p>Upon opening his eyes, Khwaja Muinuddin found they had crossed the river. This incident underscored the master's grace and the power attained through spiritual devotion.</p><p>His travels included pilgrimages to Mecca and visits to the Rawdah Mubarak of the Holy Prophet in Medina (SAW). While in Mecca, Khwaja Usman Harooni pronounced an oath, declaring Khwaja Muinuddin as one of God’s Beloved</p><p>They almost travelled the whole whold world and every Islamic city and he also went to baghdad and met Shaykh Abdul Qadir Jilani and got spiritual blessings from him who was also his cousin.</p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">The reason behind his journey to India:</span></p><p>This period of asceticism and travel prepared Khwaja Muinuddin for his subsequent Divine call to proceed to India, He was once in Medina standing near the Rawdah Mubarak and was reminded of his hadith: "I feel cool breezes coming to me from the land of Hind".</p><p>Then he realized its his destiny and he is the breeze which has to go to India and spread Islam there so he started his journey to India.</p><p><br /></p><p>During his travel to India, Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti passed through the town of Ray (between Harwan and Baghdad), where there was a major center for fire-worshipers (Magians/Zoroastrians).</p><p>There was a big fire temple in the town, where 20 wagon-loads of firewood were consumed daily to keep the fire alive for 24 hours.</p><p>The Khwaja stayed in a nearby shed under a shady tree.</p><p>One evening, he asked his attendant, Fakhruddin, to arrange for fire and prepare some food to end the day’s fast.</p><p>Fakhruddin went to the fire-worshippers and begged for some burning coal, but they refused to give him any fire.</p><p>When the incident was reported to Khwaja Saheb, he smiled and said, "This is our God, we cannot give any fire out of it.".</p><p>Khwaja Muinuddin then prayed to God. Immediately, the great fire in the temple died out.</p><p>Or some sources say he went and sat in the fire by making the magians promise that if he survives in that fire they will convert to Islam so it happened he sat in that fire for hours and even prayed and came out so they were shocked to see such a miracle and converted to Islam.</p><p>The fire-worshippers, seeing this miracle, embraced Islam and became devotees of Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti.</p><p><br /></p><p>He then made his first stop in India at Lahore where he lived and worked for forty days. Khwaja Saheb eventually entered India and arrived in Ajmer in 1191 A.D. (587 A.H.). His mission was to spread the doctrine of "Universal Brotherhood" and the sacred message of love and peace.</p><p><br /></p><p>Ajmer (The Mission Center): He then proceeded toward Delhi and Multan, but eventually settled down at Ajmer. He chose Ajmer because it was considered an “ideal centre” for his spiritual mission. Politically, Ajmer was a pivot of a mighty kingdom and geographically, it was deemed the “heart of India”.</p><p><br /></p><p>Upon arriving in Ajmer, the Khwaja Saheb and his few companions (disciples) immediately encountered resistance and prejudice.</p><p><br /></p><p>Khwaja Muinuddin arrived in Ajmer during the reign of Raja Prithviraj Chauhan.</p><p>He and his followers chose a place for their stay outside the city wall, near the Anasagar lake. This spot happened to be the stabling ground for the Raja’s camels.</p><p>The Raja's camel drivers, who were “arrogantly encamped” there, forced Khwaja Muinuddin to move to another site.</p><p>Following this act of injustice, a major spiritual incident (miracle, or karamaat) occurred. The camel drivers “did not get up next morning in spite of all the repeated efforts to make them move”.</p><p>Furthermore, the milk in the breasts of all nursing mothers and the teats of the animals of the city was dried up miraculously. This unusual phenomenon “caused an unprecedented public commotion and an unmerited shock” in the city.</p><p>When Raja Prithviraj was informed, he decided to deal with the matter with respect. He sent a message to the Khwaja Saheb asking for help, but the Khwaja Saheb simply replied: “"Let us first see what status you hold in the realm of ‘Spiritual World.’ After that, God willing, we shall also show you our position."”.</p><p>This series of events led to the miraculous conversion of the Head Priest, Shadi Dev, and the famous yogi, Ajaipal Yogi, who became his devoted disciples.</p><p><br /></p><p>This was cited as the saint's "first miracle on the soil of India," which quickly removed prejudice.</p><p><br /></p><p>1. The Head Priest, Shadi Dev (or Sadhu Ram), seeing the power of the Khwaja, became convinced of his message and embraced Islam. He is considered the saint's "first converted disciple in Ajmer".</p><p>2. Conversion of Ajaipal Yogi: The famous yogi, Ajaipal Yogi, initiated several magical attacks against the Khwaja Saheb and his companions.</p><p>Attack with Chakras: Ajaipal first launched “a violent attack through a whirlwind of ‘chakras’ (a sharp-edged disc that terribly revolved in the air and cut everything it touched)”. Khwaja Muinuddin simply threw his wooden sandals at the chakras, causing the discs to “all re-acted back on Ajaipal’s own men and caused no harm at the saint’s party”.</p><p>Attack with Beasts: The second attack involved a “huge army of horrible boas, venomous snakes and scorpions”.</p><p>Surrender: After his attempts failed repeatedly, Ajaipal became frustrated. He eventually “humbly approached the Khwaja Saheb and begged his mercy for the thinking population”. Ajaipal surrendered himself and “surrendered his mind and soul to the mercy of Khwaja Saheb”. He became a devoted disciple and was given the Islamic name Abdulla Bayabani.</p><p><br /></p><p>Raja Prithviraj and his people were trying to do everything to make Khwaja Moinuddin go away from ajmer and were trying to annoy him but there was no response from the side of Khwaja.</p><p>The Grave Words: When Raja Prithviraj’s officials arrogantly encamped near the Khwaja Saheb, the Khwaja later heard the proceedings of the Raja's council. He “simply exclaimed these 15 words "We have arrested the Raja alive and handed him over to the army of Islam."</p><p><br /></p><p>The Khwaja Saheb's words, though seeming baseless at the time, were fulfilled. Shahabuddin Ghauri, who had previously suffered a “disgraceful defeat” in the first battle of Tarain (587 A.H. or 1191 A.D.), won a crucial victory in the second battle. This victory is explicitly attributed to the “blessings of Khwaja Muinuddin”</p><p>The First Battle Ghauri lost was before the words of Khwaja Mouinuddin but the second one was after these blessed words from his mouth and Ghauri won this time.</p><p><br /></p><p>He is said to have converted atleast 9 million people during his life time.</p><p><br /></p><p>Once Khwaja Muinuddin was present when his beloved disciple, Khwaja Qutubuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, arrived at a well in Ajmer which was “quite dry”.</p><p>Khwaja Qutubuddin wished to perform his ablutions (Wazoo).</p><p>Khwaja Muinuddin “simply smiled and drew water from the dry well,” and immediately the well "became full of water". This astonishment was reported by the disciples.</p><p><br /></p><p>His Karramat are too many to even mention here they are just too much he is said to have performed more than five thousand karramat in his life and many of them have been recorded by various historians and many scholars.</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">End-Life Events and Manner of Passing.</span></p><p>The last night of Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti's life was characterized by deep spiritual contemplation, leading to his tranquil passing.</p><p>1. The Instruction to Devotees: On his final night, he instructed his devotees to close the door of his cell and not disturb him.</p><p>2. State of Ecstasy (Wajd): During the early hours of the morning, his devotees “heard a noise of something like ‘wajd’ (ecstacy) throughout that particular night” coming from inside the room1.</p><p>3. The Final Moment: The following morning, when the door did not open at the time of the morning prayers (5 to 6 A.M.), it was forced open. The disciples were astonished to find that the saint had “relinquished his mortal remains”.</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">The Miracle of the Forehead Inscription:</span></p><p>The most profound spiritual sign (Karamaat) associated with his death was the inscription found upon his forehead:</p><p>When the devotees found his mortal remains, they noticed the following sentence in Arabic “was radiantly glittering on his forehead”: “Haaza Habib-Allah Mata Fee Hoob-Allah”</p><p>This translates to: "He is God's beloved and he died in God's love".</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">Prophetic Vision of the Holy Prophet.</span></p><p>A confirmed account relates a dream experienced by his spiritual successor, Khwaja Qutubuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki, after Khwaja Muinuddin’s demise:</p><p>Khwaja Qutubuddin saw Hazrat Khwaja Muinuddin standing near Arsh-e-Moalla (the high throne of the Almighty God).</p><p>The author of 'Rahat-ul-Quloob' recorded that on the night of Khwaja Saheb's 'wisaal' (final union with God), certain Wali-Allahs (holy saints) were in communion with God and heard the Holy Prophet Muhammad saying: "Muinuddin is a beloved friend of God, we are going for his reception".</p><p><br /></p><p><span style="font-size: medium;">Date, Place, and Age of Death:</span></p><p>Khwaja Muinuddin Chishti passed away, dying in God's love, in Ajmer.</p><p>Date and Time: His precise date of death is given as the 6th of Rajab, 633 A.H.45. This corresponds to the year 1255 A.D.</p><p>Some records suggest the date was the 6th of Rajab, 627 A.H. (21st May 1229 A.D.), but the majority of reliable sources agree on 633 A.H.</p><p>He passed away during the prescribed time of the ‘Isha’ prayer, which is noted as between 8:30 P.M. and midnight.</p><p>Age: He lived to the age of 97 years.</p>
