## The Story of Mūsā AS and al-Khiḍr
Updated on: 2026-08-24T19:37:49.532Z
The Story of Mūsā AS and al-Khiḍr: The Limits of Intellect Before Divine Revelation
One of the clearest demonstrations that human reason cannot independently and infallibly determine the complete moral and legal status of every particular act is found in the Qur’ānic account of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr.
Abū Manṣūr al-Māturīdī himself establishes an important epistemological principle when discussing the limitations of human intellects:
“The fundamental principle is that intellects are finite creations which fall short of comprehending the totality of things, and understandings are deficient in attaining the ultimate reality of matters.… Whoever seeks to comprehend them, or to attain the wisdom of Lordship without an indication from Him, wrongs the intellect and burdens it with that which he knows it is incapable of.”
— al-Māturīdī, Taʾwīlāt Ahl al-Sunna, vol. 4, p. 457.
This principle becomes particularly evident in the story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr.
When al-Khiḍr killed the boy, Mūsā immediately objected:
“Have you killed an innocent soul without [its having killed] another soul? You have certainly done a terrible thing.”
Qur’ān 18:74
From everything that was outwardly apparent to Mūsā, the act was impermissible. The boy appeared to be an innocent person, no offence warranting his execution had been publicly established, and Mūsā had not been given any revealed command permitting him to be killed.
Ibn Baṭṭāl makes the epistemological significance of this episode particularly explicit. He writes that Mūsā’s objection was correct according to what was outwardly apparent, and that Mūsā was not blameworthy for making the objection. Yet when al-Khiḍr subsequently disclosed the concealed reality behind his action, the matter appeared differently:
“Mūsā’s denial of al-Khiḍr for making a hole in the ship and killing the boy was correct according to what was outwardly apparent, and Mūsā was not blameworthy for that. Then, when al-Khiḍr explained its reason and meaning, the correctness that had appeared to Mūsā in his objection became error, and the error that had appeared to Mūsā in al-Khiḍr’s action became correctness.”
— Ibn Baṭṭāl, Sharḥ Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, 1:200.
The Qur’ān then reveals the information that had been hidden from Mūsā:
“As for the boy, his parents were believers, and We feared that he would overwhelm them with transgression and disbelief. So We desired that their Lord should replace him with one better than him in purity and nearer in affection. And I did not do it on my own authority.”
Qur’ān 18:80–82
The final statement is especially significant:
“And I did not do it on my own authority.”
Al-Khiḍr therefore did not simply follow a personal moral intuition or exercise an independent judgment contrary to revelation. His action rested upon a divine command and knowledge of circumstances concealed from Mūsā.
This gives us an important distinction.
Mūsā judged according to the outwardly available evidence. Given what he knew, his objection was entirely understandable and, as Ibn Baṭṭāl explicitly states, correct according to the apparent circumstances. But Mūsā did not possess all of the relevant information concerning the boy, his future effect upon his believing parents, and the divine wisdom connected with his death.
Once the concealed information and divine authorization were disclosed, the apparent assessment could no longer be treated as the complete ruling concerning the matter.
Thus, the episode demonstrates that there is a difference between:
what appears to the intellect from the information available to it; and
the complete reality of a matter as known and commanded by Allah.
This is precisely where the limitation of finite human intellect becomes relevant.
The intellect may recognize general principles, reason from available evidence, and judge according to what is apparent. But it cannot claim exhaustive knowledge of every hidden fact, every future consequence, every particular circumstance, and every aspect of divine wisdom. If those hidden realities can affect the ruling of a particular matter, then unaided intellect cannot make itself the final and infallible judge over revelation.
Ibn Baṭṭāl therefore draws a broader theological principle from the story:
Whatever Allah has made binding upon His servants through His religion and Law must be authoritative over the intellects; the intellects are not themselves to be made the authority over it.
He then warns against making the finite intellect the ultimate judge over matters whose realities it cannot encompass. Created intellects have an end, whereas Allah’s knowledge has no end:
“They encompass nothing of His knowledge except what He wills.”
Qur’ān 2:255
The argument is therefore not that reason is useless, irrational, or incapable of recognizing anything good or evil. Rather, the argument is that reason is not omniscient. Its judgments concerning particular acts depend upon the information available to it. Revelation, however, may disclose a fact, command, exception, or divine wisdom that the intellect could not have reached independently.
The story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr provides an unusually powerful illustration of this principle. Mūsā’s initial judgment was based upon the outward reality available to him; al-Khiḍr possessed additional divinely disclosed knowledge. The difference was therefore not that Mūsā suddenly became irrational and al-Khiḍr suddenly became rational. Rather, the epistemic circumstances were different.
This is also consistent with al-Māturīdī’s own admission that finite intellects cannot comprehend the totality of things or attain the ultimate realities and divine wisdoms independently of divine indication. If the intellect itself is acknowledged to be finite, then it cannot legitimately transform its own limited assessment into an infallible standard by which every divine command must be judged.
Accordingly, when someone argues:
“Allah could not command this particular act because my intellect determines that it is intrinsically immoral,”
the question that must first be asked is:
How do you know that your intellect possesses all the facts and circumstances relevant to the divine ruling?
The story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr demonstrates precisely why such certainty cannot simply be assumed. Mūsā saw an apparently innocent person being killed and objected according to the outward evidence. Yet a concealed divine command and concealed knowledge altered the complete understanding of the event.
Therefore, the proper conclusion is not that revelation contradicts sound reason. Rather, it is that finite reason cannot claim exhaustive knowledge of every particular act or every divine wisdom, and consequently cannot establish itself as an infallible judge over revelation.
The intellect has a place: it understands, reasons, recognizes evidence, and receives revelation. But when revelation establishes something whose complete wisdom lies beyond its apprehension, the proper response is not to reject the revelation because the intellect has failed to comprehend its entirety. The proper response is to recognize the limitation of the intellect itself.
This is the fundamental lesson Ibn Baṭṭāl extracts from the story of Mūsā and al-Khiḍr what appears irrational or impermissible to a finite intellect on the basis of incomplete information cannot thereby be declared impossible for Allah to command, particularly when revelation supplies the information and authority that the intellect itself lacked.
## Ahadith on the Virtues of Sending Salawat upon the Prophet (S) on Friday.
Updated on: 2026-08-24T21:07:28Z
Abu Bakr ibn Abi Shaybah narrated to us, Al-Husayn ibn Ali narrated to us, from Abd al-Rahman ibn Yazid ibn Jabir, from Abu al-Ash'ath al-San'ani, from Shaddad ibn Aws, who said: The Messenger of Allah (peace be upon him) said, "Indeed, among the best of your days is Friday; on it, Adam was created, on it is the blowing of the trumpet, and on it is the swoon. So increase your prayers upon me on it, for your prayers are presented to me." A man said, "O Messenger of Allah, how are our prayers presented to you when you have decayed?" He said, "Indeed, Allah has forbidden the earth to consume the bodies of the prophets."

(Ibn Majah 1085)

On the authority of Aws ibn Aws (may Allah be pleased with him), he said: The Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) said: "Among the best of your days is Friday, so increase your prayers upon me on that day; for your prayers are presented to me."

(Sunan Abi Dawud 1531, Ibn Majah 1636, Sunan Al Kubara of An Nasa'i 1666)

Narrated by Abu Huraira (1).

Narrated by Abu Huraira (2).

The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever sends blessings upon me at my grave, I hear him, and whoever sends blessings upon me from afar, an angel is appointed to convey it to me, and it suffices for his worldly and hereafter affairs, and I will be a witness or an intercessor for him."

Hib, and Al-Khatib from Abu Huraira (3).

The Prophet (S) said: "Whoever sends blessings upon me on Friday, it will be an intercession for him with me on the Day of Resurrection."

Al-Daylami from Aisha (SA).

Narrated by Abu Abdullah Muhammad bin Abdullah Al-Hafiz, in Nishapur, from Muhammad bin Abi Mu'adh from his father, from Ibrahim bin Adham, from Muhammad bin Ajlan, from Ali bin Al-Husayn, from his father, from Ali bin Abi Talib, who said: The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "Whoever sends blessings upon me one hundred times on Friday will come on the Day of Resurrection with a light that, if distributed among all creation, would suffice them."

(Hilayat Awliya)

Allah appoints an angel to deliver it to my grave just as gifts are delivered to one of you. My knowledge after my death is like my knowledge during my life."

(Al-Daylami from Hakama from her father Uthman bin Dinar from his brother Malik bin Dinar from Anas)."

Also narrated by: Al-Bayhaqi in Shu'ab al-Iman (3/111, No. 3035).

This is a Sahih Hadith and the best one about the Prophet (S) still having the same knowledge which Allah (SWT) granted him during his blessed life.

"The second characteristic: It is recommended to frequently send blessings upon the Prophet - peace be upon him - on this day and its night, for he said: "Increase your prayers upon me on Friday and the night of Friday".

The Messenger of Allah - peace be upon him - is the master of mankind, and Friday is the master of days. Therefore, sending prayers upon him on this day has a special merit not found on other days. Another wisdom is that every good his nation received in this world and the hereafter was through him, as Allah combined for his nation the good of this world and the hereafter through him. The greatest honor they receive will occur on Friday, as it is the day they are sent to their homes and palaces in paradise, and it is the day of increase for them when they enter paradise. It is a holiday for them in this world, and a day when Allah Almighty fulfills their requests and needs, and does not turn away their supplications. All of this was known and achieved because of him and through him. Therefore, out of gratitude, praise, and fulfilling a small part of his due right of him- ﷺ - it is encouraged to frequently send prayers upon him on this day and its night."

(Zaad Al Maa'd 1/461)

## Explanation of free will
Updated on: 2026-08-24T20:53:35.695Z
 1. The Core Distinction: Creator vs. Acquirer

The first step is to establish that every action has two "facets." Allah is the Creator (Khāliq) of the action because only He can bring something from non-existence into existence. However, the human is the Acquirer (Kāsib).

The Analogy: When a slave expends their power and will toward an action, that is kasb. When Allah brings that action into existence following that intent, that is khalq.

The Ruling: Praise, blame, reward, and punishment are attached to the Acquisition, not the creation. Allah creates the "movement," but the human provides the "intent" (e.g., the hand's movement is created by God, but the choice to use it for charity or theft belongs to the human).

2. The "Partial Will": Where Freedom Lives

A common question is: "If God created my will, how am I free?" The answer lies in the Partial Will (al-irāda al-juz’iyya) or Sincere Resolve (‘azm muṣammam),

We distinguish between "Inclination" (which can be created and influenced) and "Intention" (the actual decision).

This "Partial Will" is considered a Relational State (amr i’tibārī) or a State (ḥāl). It is neither a physically "existent thing" nor "nothing"; it is a point of entry (madkhal) that does not fall under the category of "creation" (khalq) in the material sense.

Because this resolve is not a "thing" created by God, it is the one area where the human is truly independent and held responsible. It is the "trigger" that occasions God's creation of the act.

3. Capacity (Istitā‘a): When are we able to act?

To avoid contradiction, you must explain that there are two types of ability:

General Ability: This is the soundness of limbs and means (being healthy, having hands to work). This exists before the action and is the basis for being held responsible (taklīf).

Actual Capacity: This is the specific power to execute a specific act. This is created by Allah simultaneously with the action (ma’a al-fi‘l).

Allah creates the power to do good if the slave intends good, and the power to do evil if the slave intends evil.

Thus, the human "wastes" the potential for good by choosing the resolve for evil.

4. Knowledge is not Compulsion

The final hurdle is usually the argument: "If God knew eternally I would do this, I must do it."

In our theology, Knowledge is a Follower (tābi‘) of the thing known. God knows what will happen as it will happen,

He knows that you will perform a certain act by your own choice. If He knew you were forced, and you weren't, His knowledge would be "ignorance," which is impossible.

Knowledge is a "disclosure" (inkishāf), not a "necessitating cause" (‘illa mūjiba). Knowing a car will crash because its brakes are cut doesn't mean your knowledge caused the crash.

Allah has given us the capacity to do all He has made a duty. If He took away the capacity, the duty would fall away. We choose the 'direction' of our hearts, and Allah, the Wise, creates the 'reality' of those choices to test our true nature.
